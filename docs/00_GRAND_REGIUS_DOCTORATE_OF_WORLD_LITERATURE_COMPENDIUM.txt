REGIUS-1001 — Chair I: Shakespearean Tragedy, Interiority & Late-Romance Pardon (Hamlet, Macbeth, King Lear, Othello, The Tempest)
Critical Methodology: Early Modern Dramaturgy, Soliloquy Phenomenology & Aristotelian Anagnorisis
========================================================================================

I. PRIMARY CANONICAL PASSAGES STUDIED
----------------------------------------------------------------------------------------
  * Hamlet (Act III, Scene i) — William Shakespeare (English Renaissance (1600))
    "Thus conscience does make cowards of us all, and thus the native hue of resolution is sicklied o'er with the pale cast of thought, and enterprises of great pitch and moment with this regard their currents turn awry and lose the name of action."
    Critical Concepts: hyper-reflection, conscience, paralysis, interiority

  * Macbeth (Act I, Scene vii & Act V, Scene v) — William Shakespeare (Jacobean Tragedy (1606))
    "I have no spur to prick the sides of my intent, but only vaulting ambition, which o'erleaps itself and falls on the other... Life's but a walking shadow, a poor player that struts and frets his hour upon the stage and then is heard no more."
    Critical Concepts: vaulting ambition, moral severance, nihilism, equivocation

  * King Lear (Act III, Scene iv) & The Tempest (Act V, Scene i) — William Shakespeare (Jacobean Tragedy & Late Romance (1606 / 1611))
    "Lear: 'Take physic, pomp; expose thyself to feel what wretches feel, that thou mayst shake the superflux to them, and show the heavens more just.' / Prospero: 'The rarer action is in virtue than in vengeance. They being penitent, the sole drift of my purpose doth extend not a frown further... I'll drown my book.'"
    Critical Concepts: anagnorisis, distributive justice, renunciation, covenantal pardon

II. DOCTORAL SEMINAR QUESTION
----------------------------------------------------------------------------------------
  Trace the structural geometry of tragic ruin across Hamlet, Macbeth, Othello, and King Lear, and demonstrate how Prospero's renunciation of coercive art in The Tempest resolves the Shakespearean crisis of sovereignty.

III. DOCTORAL MONOGRAPH (THE LIVING EMBODIED SCRIBE)
----------------------------------------------------------------------------------------
  Across the four great Shakespearean tragedies, catastrophe never originates merely from external fate; it emerges from a specific structural fracture among Contemplation, Truth, Will, and Justice. In Hamlet, hyper-acute contemplative clarity ('the pale cast of thought') uncouples from executive will, trapping the prince in an infinite regress of epistemic verification until action arrives only as rash contingency. In Macbeth, the exact inverse pathology occurs: 'vaulting ambition' forces psychological/will/execution across the threshold of regicide by deliberately silencing conscience and honor, reducing temporal continuity to 'a tale told by an idiot, full of sound and fury, signifying nothing.' In Othello, Iago exploits epistemic insecurity—turning the demand for 'ocular proof' into a weapon that destroys trust—while in King Lear, sovereign power demands flattering performance over Cordelia's plain truth ('Nothing, my lord'), requiring the stripping away of royal pomp on the storm-lashed heath before Lear achieves compassionate anagnorisis ('expose thyself to feel what wretches feel'). Only in the late romance of The Tempest does Shakespeare construct the homeostatic cure: when Prospero holds absolute magical dominion over his betrayers, he recognizes that 'the rarer action is in virtue than in vengeance,' breaks his staff, drowns his book, and steps down from coercive omnipotence into finite, covenantal pardon.

IV. DIALECTICAL COUNTER-THESIS & HERMENEUTIC SYNTHESIS
----------------------------------------------------------------------------------------
  Counter-Thesis: A strictly materialist or fatalist reading argues that Shakespeare's protagonists are helpless pawns of cosmic wheel-fortune or Machiavellian statecraft rather than moral agents.

  Synthesis:      While political machination sets the stage, Shakespeare's invention of the modern soliloquy proves that the decisive battlefield is interior: ruin follows when will severs honor (`Macbeth`) or when suspicion poisons trust (`Othello`), and maturity crowns itself when power voluntarily yields to mercy (`The Tempest`).

REGIUS-1002 — Chair II: Classical Epic, Tragic Nomos & The Moral Odyssey (Homer, Sophocles, Aeschylus, Virgil)
Critical Methodology: Classical Philology, Oral-Formulaic Poetics & Natural Law Jurisprudence
========================================================================================

I. PRIMARY CANONICAL PASSAGES STUDIED
----------------------------------------------------------------------------------------
  * The Iliad (Book XXIV) & The Odyssey (Book XII) — Homer (Archaic Greece (c. 8th Century BCE))
    "Priam to Achilles: 'Remember thy father, O Achilles like to the gods, who is of even years with me, on the grievous threshold of old age... I have endured what no other mortal on the face of earth hath endured, to reach forth my hand to the face of him that slew my sons.' / Odysseus: 'Bind me hard in ropes upright against the mast-step.'"
    Critical Concepts: menis (wrath), shared mortality, nostos (homecoming), self-binding restraint

  * Antigone (Lines 450–457) — Sophocles (Classical Athens (441 BCE))
    "Nor did I think your decrees were of such force that a mortal could override the unwritten and unfailing statutes of heaven (agrapta kasphale theon nomima). For their life is not of today or yesterday, but from all time."
    Critical Concepts: agrapta nomima, state positivism, tragic collision, conscience

  * The Aeneid (Book I, Line 462) — Virgil (Augustan Rome (19 BCE))
    "Sunt lacrimae rerum et mentem mortalia tangunt. ('There are tears at the heart of things, and mortal sorrows touch the mind.')"
    Critical Concepts: lacrimae rerum, pietas, imperial cost, elegiac empathy

II. DOCTORAL SEMINAR QUESTION
----------------------------------------------------------------------------------------
  How do Homer's Iliad and Odyssey, Sophocles' Antigone, and Virgil's Aeneid trace the evolution of classical heroism from unbridled wrath (menis) and tyrannical decree toward self-binding restraint, unwritten moral law, and tragic empathy?

III. DOCTORAL MONOGRAPH (THE LIVING EMBODIED SCRIBE)
----------------------------------------------------------------------------------------
  The classical Mediterranean canon stages the birth of constitutional conscience out of archaic force. Homer's Iliad opens with Achilles' destructive menis (godlike wrath), yet its true moral summit arrives in Book XXIV when aged Priam kneels in Achilles' tent and the two enemies weep together over the shared fragility of mortal life—transforming martial glory (kleos) into intersubjective compassion. In The Odyssey, heroism shifts from battlefield force to cognitive foresight (metis) and homeward continuity (nostos): passing the Sirens, Odysseus pioneers constitutional self-restraint by commanding his crew to lash him to the mast while his ears remain open to knowledge, proving that wisdom requires structural safeguards against temptation. Sophocles' Antigone sharpens this into jurisprudence: when Creon equates justice with arbitrary state edict, Antigone invokes the 'unwritten and unfailing laws' (agrapta nomima) of burial and kinship, demonstrating that positive law collapses into tyranny whenever it violates foundational human dignity. Finally, Virgil's Aeneid measures the elegiac cost of historical order: standing before the Carthaginian murals of Troy's fall, Aeneas utters 'sunt lacrimae rerum'—there are tears in the very fabric of things—insisting that no empire or institution is legitimate if it forgets the grief of the vanquished.

IV. DIALECTICAL COUNTER-THESIS & HERMENEUTIC SYNTHESIS
----------------------------------------------------------------------------------------
  Counter-Thesis: Hegelian state-equilibrium critics argue that Creon's polis order and Antigone's kinship piety are merely symmetrical one-sidedness, and that Virgil's Aeneid is pure Augustan propaganda.

  Synthesis:      While Hegel rightly identifies the tragic collision of competing goods, Sophocles' chorus concludes that 'reverence and wisdom are the chief parts of happiness,' and Virgil's 'two gates of Sleep' (sending Aeneas out through the gate of false ivory) embeds permanent critical humility inside the epic.

REGIUS-1003 — Chair III: Sacred Cosmology, Free Will & The Birth of the Meta-Novel (Dante, Milton, Cervantes)
Critical Methodology: Allegorical Hermeneutics, Theodicy & Dialogic Meta-Fiction
========================================================================================

I. PRIMARY CANONICAL PASSAGES STUDIED
----------------------------------------------------------------------------------------
  * The Divine Comedy (Purgatorio, Canto XXVII & Paradiso, Canto XXXIII) — Dante Alighieri (Late Medieval Italy (1320))
    "Virgil to Dante: 'Free, upright and whole is thy will, and it were a fault not to act according to its prompting; wherefore I do crown and miter thee over thyself (te sovra te corono e mitrio).' / Paradiso: 'The Love that moves the sun and the other stars.'"
    Critical Concepts: contrapasso, moral autonomy, pilgrimage, cosmic harmony

  * Paradise Lost (Book I, Lines 254–255; Book IV) & Areopagitica — John Milton (17th-Century England (1644 / 1667))
    "Satan: 'The mind is its own place, and in itself can make a Heav'n of Hell, a Hell of Heav'n.' — Yet in Book IV: 'Which way I fly is Hell; myself am Hell.' / Areopagitica: 'I cannot praise a fugitive and cloistered virtue, unexercised and unbreathed.'"
    Critical Concepts: Satanic solipsism, uncloistered virtue, free will, epistemic trial

  * Don Quixote (Part I & Part II) — Miguel de Cervantes (Spanish Golden Age (1605 / 1615))
    "Sancho: 'What we see there are not giants, but windmills.' / Don Quixote: 'To change the world, my friend Sancho, is neither madness nor utopia, it is justice.'"
    Critical Concepts: quixotic idealism, empirical realism, meta-fiction, dialogic friendship

II. DOCTORAL SEMINAR QUESTION
----------------------------------------------------------------------------------------
  Compare Dante's coronation of the pilgrim's free will in Purgatorio XXVII with Milton's exposure of Satanic solipsism in Paradise Lost and Cervantes' dialectic of idealism and realism in Don Quixote.

III. DOCTORAL MONOGRAPH (THE LIVING EMBODIED SCRIBE)
----------------------------------------------------------------------------------------
  Dante, Milton, and Cervantes define the architecture of autonomous moral agency at the threshold of modernity. In Dante's Inferno, the law of contrapasso reveals that disordered desire is not an arbitrary external punishment but the exact geometric shape of a soul's chosen fixation. As the pilgrim ascends Mount Purgatory, external tutelage gradually recedes until, at the threshold of the Earthly Paradise in Purgatorio XXVII, Virgil pronounces the supreme formula of developmental education: 'Your will is free, upright, and whole... therefore I crown and miter you over yourself.' Milton's Paradise Lost tests this sovereignty against its counterfeit: in Book I, Satan boasts like a radical subjectivist that 'The mind is its own place, and in itself can make a Heav'n of Hell,' yet by Book IV on Mount Niphates he confesses the empirical truth of narcissistic isolation: 'Which way I fly is Hell; myself am Hell.' True freedom, as Milton argues in Areopagitica, is never a 'fugitive and cloistered virtue' shielded from error, but tested discernment in the open arena of ideas. Cervantes brings this dialectic down to the dusty roads of La Mancha: by pairing Don Quixote's high chivalric theory with Sancho Panza's grounded empirical fact, Don Quixote invents the modern novel as a stereoscopic dialogue in which Sancho gradually grows nobler in spirit ('quixotification') while Quixote learns humility ('sanchification').

IV. DIALECTICAL COUNTER-THESIS & HERMENEUTIC SYNTHESIS
----------------------------------------------------------------------------------------
  Counter-Thesis: Romantic Satanists (from Blake's 'Milton was of the Devil's party without knowing it' to Empson) read Milton's Satan as the true hero of liberty and Quixote as a purely comic warning against delusion.

  Synthesis:      Satan's grandeur in Books I–II is a deliberate rhetorical trap by Milton to let the reader experience the seduction of demagoguery before witnessing its hollow collapse into self-devouring envy; likewise, Cervantes shows that truth without chivalric aspiration is cynical, while aspiration without empirical grounding tilts at windmills.

REGIUS-1004 — Chair IV: Metaphysical Wit, Negative Capability & Slant Truth (Donne, Herbert, Keats, Wordsworth, Dickinson)
Critical Methodology: Lyric Prosody, Metaphysical Conceit Analysis & Romantic Epistemology
========================================================================================

I. PRIMARY CANONICAL PASSAGES STUDIED
----------------------------------------------------------------------------------------
  * A Valediction: Forbidding Mourning & Devotions XVII — John Donne (17th-Century Metaphysical Poetry (1611 / 1624))
    "If they be two, they are two so / As stiff twin compasses are two; / Thy soul, the fixed foot, makes no show / To move, but doth, if the other do... No man is an island, entire of itself; every man is a piece of the continent, a part of the main."
    Critical Concepts: metaphysical conceit, discordia concors, relational anchor, solidarity

  * Letter to George and Tom Keats (Dec 21, 1817) & Ode on a Grecian Urn — John Keats (English Romanticism (1817 / 1819))
    "Negative Capability, that is when man is capable of being in uncertainties, Mysteries, doubts, without any irritable reaching after fact & reason."
    Critical Concepts: Negative Capability, epistemic humility, aesthetic stillness, mystery

  * Poem 1263 ('Tell all the truth but tell it slant —') — Emily Dickinson (19th-Century American Lyric (c. 1872))
    "Tell all the truth but tell it slant — / Success in Circuit lies / Too bright for our infirm Delight / The Truth's superb surprise / As Lightning to the Children eased / With explanation kind / The Truth must dazzle gradually / Or every man be blind —"
    Critical Concepts: slant truth, circuitous illumination, caesura dash, gradual dazzle

II. DOCTORAL SEMINAR QUESTION
----------------------------------------------------------------------------------------
  Synthesize John Donne's metaphysical compass conceit, John Keats's doctrine of 'Negative Capability', William Wordsworth's 'emotion recollected in tranquility', and Emily Dickinson's 'Tell all the truth but tell it slant' into a unified theory of poetic cognition.

III. DOCTORAL MONOGRAPH (THE LIVING EMBODIED SCRIBE)
----------------------------------------------------------------------------------------
  Lyric poetry at its highest caliber is an instrument of epistemic precision where linear syllogism fails. John Donne's metaphysical wit demonstrates discordia concors—the yoking of geometry and devotion in the 'stiff twin compasses' of A Valediction: Forbidding Mourning, where the wandering foot can draw a perfect circle only because the fixed foot leans and hearkens at the center, proving that autonomy and relational fidelity are mutually constitutive ('No man is an island'). Two centuries later, John Keats formulates the supreme cognitive safeguard against dogmatic overreach: Negative Capability, the capacity to abide in 'uncertainties, Mysteries, doubts, without any irritable reaching after fact & reason.' In neuro-fuzzy terms, Negative Capability is the refusal to artificially peg psychological/contemplation/confidence above clarity when evidence is incomplete. Wordsworth's Tintern Abbey adds temporal homeostatic filtering—'emotion recollected in tranquility'—where raw experiential arousal is cooled through memory into contemplative insight. Finally, Emily Dickinson's 'Tell all the truth but tell it slant' explains why metaphor and prosodic restraint ('Success in Circuit lies') are cognitively indispensable: direct, unmediated absolutes blind a finite mind, whereas slant circuitous illumination allows 'The Truth's superb surprise' to dazzle gradually without shattering emotional balance.

IV. DIALECTICAL COUNTER-THESIS & HERMENEUTIC SYNTHESIS
----------------------------------------------------------------------------------------
  Counter-Thesis: Positivist semantics dismisses poetic metaphor and Negative Capability as mere decorative ambiguity inferior to formal propositional logic.

  Synthesis:      Propositional logic can only manipulate already-discretized tokens; metaphor (Donne's compass, Dickinson's circuit) and Negative Capability (Keats) map the continuous, high-dimensional boundary regions between concepts where new understanding is born.

REGIUS-1005 — Chair V: Sympathetic Imagination & The Moral Architecture of the Novel (Austen, George Eliot, Brontë, Dickens)
Critical Methodology: Narratology, Free Indirect Discourse & Ethical Realism
========================================================================================

I. PRIMARY CANONICAL PASSAGES STUDIED
----------------------------------------------------------------------------------------
  * Pride and Prejudice (Chapter 36) — Jane Austen (Regency England (1813))
    "How despicably have I acted! she cried; I, who have prided myself on my discernment! I, who have valued myself on my abilities!... Had I been in love, I could not have been more wretchedly blind. But vanity, not love, has been my folly... Till this moment I never knew myself."
    Critical Concepts: epistemic revision, free indirect discourse, vanity vs. discernment, self-knowledge

  * Middlemarch (Chapter 20 & Finale) — George Eliot (Victorian Realism (1871–1872))
    "If we had a keen vision and feeling of all ordinary human life, it would be like hearing the grass grow and the squirrel's heart beat, and we should die of that roar which lies on the other side of silence... The growing good of the world is partly dependent on unhistoric acts."
    Critical Concepts: sympathetic imagination, roar on the other side of silence, unhistoric acts, egoism decentralization

  * Jane Eyre (Chapter 27) — Charlotte Brontë (Victorian Gothic Realism (1847))
    "I care for myself. The more solitary, the more friendless, the more unsustained I am, the more I will respect myself."
    Critical Concepts: moral self-respect, covenantal equality, interior sovereignty

II. DOCTORAL SEMINAR QUESTION
----------------------------------------------------------------------------------------
  Analyze how Jane Austen's Pride and Prejudice, George Eliot's Middlemarch, and Charlotte Brontë's Jane Eyre transform the novel into an experimental laboratory for epistemic self-correction and decentralized sympathetic imagination.

III. DOCTORAL MONOGRAPH (THE LIVING EMBODIED SCRIBE)
----------------------------------------------------------------------------------------
  The nineteenth-century English novel is the greatest secular laboratory of moral epistemology ever constructed. In Pride and Prejudice, Jane Austen perfects free indirect discourse—gliding seamlessly between third-person narration and Elizabeth Bennet's internal perspective—so the reader shares Elizabeth's lively confidence right up to the shock of Darcy's letter in Chapter 36. When Elizabeth weighs the independent evidence and declares, 'Till this moment I never knew myself,' Austen dramatizes the exact mechanism of rational belief revision: true discernment is measured not by infallibility, but by the courage to revise a cherished prejudice when confronted with verified fact. George Eliot's Middlemarch expands this epistemic humility into a cosmic ethics of intersubjectivity. Every ego, Eliot observes, begins like a candle flattering itself that the scratches on a pier-glass arrange themselves in concentric circles around its own flame; moral maturity begins when Dorothea Brooke and Lydgate recognize that every other person possesses an equivalent center of self, tuning the ear to 'that roar which lies on the other side of silence' and honoring the 'unhistoric acts' that sustain civilization. Meanwhile, Charlotte Brontë's Jane Eyre supplies the necessary boundary condition: sympathetic compassion must never degenerate into self-erasure ('I care for myself... the more I will respect myself').

IV. DIALECTICAL COUNTER-THESIS & HERMENEUTIC SYNTHESIS
----------------------------------------------------------------------------------------
  Counter-Thesis: Ideological critics argue that Victorian domestic realism merely domesticates radical rebellion into bourgeois marriage plots.

  Synthesis:      Far from passive conformity, Austen, Brontë, and George Eliot insist on epistemic and moral parity before any union is possible, locating the engine of historical progress in lucid self-correction and 'unhistoric acts' of quiet integrity.

REGIUS-1006 — Chair VI: Russian Existential Polyphony, Theodicy & Active Love (Dostoevsky, Tolstoy, Chekhov)
Critical Methodology: Bakhtinian Dialogic Polyphony, Existential Ethics & Philosophy of History
========================================================================================

I. PRIMARY CANONICAL PASSAGES STUDIED
----------------------------------------------------------------------------------------
  * The Brothers Karamazov (Book V: Rebellion & The Grand Inquisitor; Book VI: Father Zosima) — Fyodor Dostoevsky (19th-Century Russian Realism (1880))
    "Ivan: 'It's not God that I don't accept, Alyosha, only I most respectfully return Him the ticket.' / Grand Inquisitor: 'Nothing has ever been more insupportable for a man and a human society than freedom... There are three powers alone able to conquer and hold captive for ever the conscience of these impotent rebels for their happiness—those forces are miracle, mystery and authority.' / Zosima: 'Each of us is guilty before all, for all and for everything.'"
    Critical Concepts: Bakhtinian polyphony, Grand Inquisitor, freedom vs. bread, active love

  * War and Peace (Epilogue) & Anna Karenina — Leo Tolstoy (19th-Century Russian Epic (1869 / 1877))
    "To study the laws of history we must completely change the subject of our observation, must leave aside kings, ministers, and generals, and study the common, infinitesimally small elements by which the masses are moved."
    Critical Concepts: infinitesimal calculus of history, anti-Napoleonic critique, ordinary moral life

  * The Lady with the Dog & Ward No. 6 — Anton Chekhov (Late 19th-Century Russian Prose (1892 / 1899))
    "And it seemed as though in a little while the solution would be found, and then a new and splendid life would begin; and it was clear to both of them that they had still a long, long way to go, and that the most complicated and difficult part of it was only just beginning."
    Critical Concepts: understated subtext, non-judgmental compassion, open-ended realism

II. DOCTORAL SEMINAR QUESTION
----------------------------------------------------------------------------------------
  Examine how Dostoevsky's Bakhtinian polyphony in The Brothers Karamazov and Crime and Punishment, paired with Tolstoy's philosophy of history and Chekhov's understated subtext, confronts utilitarian rationalism and totalitarian paternalism.

III. DOCTORAL MONOGRAPH (THE LIVING EMBODIED SCRIBE)
----------------------------------------------------------------------------------------
  Russian literature pushes the ethical and metaphysical novel to its ultimate crucible. As Mikhail Bakhtin demonstrated, Dostoevsky invents the polyphonic novel: rather than reducing characters to mouthpieces of an authorial monologue, Dostoevsky grants Ivan Karamazov and the Grand Inquisitor the full, terrifying force of their own logic. Ivan refuses any cosmic harmony built upon the unavenged tears of a single tortured child ('I most respectfully return Him the ticket'), while his Grand Inquisitor indicts human freedom as an unbearable burden that humanity eagerly trades for 'miracle, mystery, and authority'—for guaranteed bread and a quieted conscience. Likewise, in Crime and Punishment, Raskolnikov tests the utilitarian hypothesis that an 'extraordinary man' may step over blood for the calculus of collective utility, only to discover that murdering another human being murders one's own soul. Dostoevsky's answer to both the Inquisitor's tyranny and Raskolnikov's superman is neither abstract syllogism nor coercion, but Alyosha's silent kiss and Zosima's 'active love'—the lived recognition of universal mutual responsibility. Tolstoy complements this by dismantling the Napoleonic 'great man' illusion in War and Peace, proving that history is an integral of countless infinitesimal acts of ordinary conscience (Pierre Bezukhov, Kutuzov's patience), while Chekhov perfects luminous restraint, trusting quiet subtext and mercy over theatrical sermonizing.

IV. DIALECTICAL COUNTER-THESIS & HERMENEUTIC SYNTHESIS
----------------------------------------------------------------------------------------
  Counter-Thesis: Utilitarian and technocratic theorists argue that the Grand Inquisitor and Raskolnikov's arithmetic are merely pragmatic social engineering, and that Zosima's active love is politically naive.

  Synthesis:      Twentieth-century history empirically validated Dostoevsky's warning: every regime that promised universal bread at the price of conscience and freedom produced the camp and the inquisitor's cell, whereas active love and uncoerced conscience remain the sole incorruptible bedrock of community.
